Wave vectors: $\vec{k_1}=k_t\vec{e_x}+\beta\vec{e_z}$ and $\vec{k_2}=-k_t\vec{e_x}+\beta\vec{e_z}$

  • Norm: $k_1=k_2=k=k_0 n=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} n$
  • Components: $\beta=k\cos(\theta)$ and $k_t=k\sin(\theta)$
  • $\beta^2+k_t^2=k^2$

Plane waves

  • $\underline{E}_1(x,z,t)=E_0\exp\left(i\left(k_tx+\beta z-\omega t\right)\right)$
  • $\underline{E}_2(x,z,t)=E_0\exp\left(i\left(-k_tx+\beta z-\omega t\right)\right)$

When the two waves overlap: $$\underline{E}^{\theta,+}(x,z,t)=\underline{E}_1(x,z,t)+\underline{E}_2(x,z,t)=2E_0\cos(k_tx)\exp\left(i\left(\beta z-\omega t\right)\right)$$

  • Propagation in the z-direction with the wave vector $\beta \vec{e_z}$
  • Stationary field in the x-direction, fringe spacing: $d=\frac{\lambda}{2n\sin(\theta)}$

Resulting wave obtained by subtracting the two plane waves: $$\underline{E}^{\theta,-}(x,z,t)=\underline{E}_1(x,z,t)-\underline{E}_2(x,z,t)=2iE_0\sin(k_tx)\exp\left(i\left(\beta z-\omega t\right)\right)$$

  • Propagation in the z-direction with the wave vector $\beta \vec{e_z}$
  • Stationary field in the x-direction, fringe spacing: $d=\frac{\lambda}{2n\sin(\theta)}$
A wave propagating along the z-axis can be decomposed on the basis $\underline{E}^{\theta,\pm}$ that are called modes.

The EM field of a mode can be written as $\begin{pmatrix} \vec{\underline{E}}(x,y,z,t)\\\vec{\underline{H}}(x,y,z,t)\\\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{\underline{e}}(x,y)\\\vec{\underline{h}}(x,y)\end{pmatrix}\exp\left(i\left(\beta z-\omega t\right)\right)$ and each mode is characterized by:
  • its electric/magnetic field distribution $\vec{\underline{e}}(x,y)/\vec{\underline{h}}(x,y)$ in the transverse plane
  • its propagation constant $\beta$ or by its effective index defined as: $\boxed{n_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\beta}{k_0}}$


For a 2D uniform medium:
  • $e(x,y)=\cos(k_0\sqrt{n^2-n_{\text{eff}}^2}x)$ or $e(x,y)=\sin(k_0\sqrt{n^2-n_{\text{eff}}^2}x)$
  • $n_{\text{eff}}=n\cos(\theta) \leq n$ and $\theta$ can take on any value

There are also evanescent modes for which $\beta$ and $n_{\text{eff}}$ are purely imaginary

$$\Delta \underline{E} +k_0^2n^2 \underline {E} = 0$$ $$\left(\Delta_t + k_0^2n^2\right)\underline{e}=\beta^2\underline{e}$$ $\Longrightarrow \beta^2$ and $\underline{e}$ are respectively the eigenvalue and the eigenfunction of the differential operator $\Delta_t + k_0^2n^2$

Example of TE modes

The waveguide width is $2a=p\frac{\lambda}{2n\sin(\theta)}$ with $p\in \mathbb{N}^*$ so that: $$\theta=\arcsin\left(\frac{p\lambda}{4na}\right),\text{ } p\le\frac{4na}{\lambda}$$

$$\text{OPD}=(AB)-(CD)+\lambda=4na\sin(\theta)+\lambda=p\lambda$$ $$n_{\text{eff}}^2=n^2\left[1-\left(\frac{p\lambda}{4na}\right)^2\right]\text{ with } p\in \mathbb{N}$$
Test note
A mode is guided if:
  • Total internal reflexion (TIR) at the interface core/cladding: $i\ge \arcsin\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right) \Longrightarrow \frac{n_2}{n_1}\le \cos(\theta)\le 1 \Longrightarrow n_2\le n_{\text{eff}}\le n_1$
  • $\text{OPD}=4n_1a\sin(\theta)+2\frac{\phi_r}{k_0}=p\lambda \text{ with } p\in \mathbb{N}$


NB: $\phi_r$ is given by the Fresnel's coefficient of reflection and depends on $n_1$, $n_2$, $\theta$ and the polarization state
  • The modes are all orthogonal to one another $\Longrightarrow$ each mode propagates independently of the others
  • The set of eigenfunctions of the differential operator (guided modes + radiation modes + evanescent modes) constitutes a complete orthogonal basis of solutions of Maxwell's equations


At a given wavelength, we can project the field at $z = 0 $ on the modes basis: $$\underline{E}(x,y,z=0)=\sum_{\substack{\text{guided} \\ \text{modes}}}A_m\underline{e}_{m}(x,y)+\int_{\substack{\text{continuous} \\ \text{spectrum}}}A(\beta)\underline{e}_{\beta}(x,y)d\beta$$ where $A_m=\iint\underline{E}(x,y,z=0)\underline{e}_{m}(x,y) dx dy$ (idem for $A(\beta)$).

To obtain the field at $z=L$, each component must be multiplied by its phase term which is due to the propagation: $$\underline{E}(x,y,z=L)=\sum_{\substack{\text{guided} \\ \text{modes}}}A_m\underline{e}_{m}(x,y)e^{i\beta_m L}+\int_{\substack{\text{continuous} \\ \text{spectrum}}}A(\beta)\underline{e}_{\beta}(x,y)e^{i\beta L}d\beta$$

$\Longrightarrow$ the mode propagates whilst being attenuated due to partial reflection

$\Longrightarrow$ the mode propagates whilst being attenuated due to the tunnel effect (frustrated reflection)

  • Leaky modes propagate with attenuation: their propagation constant $\beta$ is complex. Their linear losses are $\alpha=2\text{Im}(\beta)$ (in m-1)
  • They form a discrete but infinite set of solutions

  • Leaky modes do not belong to the spectrum of the differential operator because they are not bounded:
    $\beta=\text{Re}(\beta)+i\text{Im}(\beta)$ $\Longrightarrow$ $k_t=\sqrt{k_0^2n_2^2-\beta^2}=\text{Re}(k_t)-i\text{Im}(k_t)$ in the cladding
    In the cladding $e(x)=e^{ik_t\vert x \vert}=e^{i\text{Re}(k_t)\vert x \vert}e^{\text{Im}(k_t)\vert x \vert}$
  • Leaky modes are solutions of Maxwell’s equations and can be decomposed on the continuum of modes


An electric field propagating in the fiber can be decomposed as: $$E(x,y,z)=\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n e_n(x,y)\exp(i\beta_n z)}_{\text{Guided modes}}+\underbrace{\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}a_m e_m(x,y)\exp(i\beta_m z)}_{\text{Leaky modes}}+E_{res}$$ The residual field can be decomposed on the continuum of modes: $E_{res}=\int\varepsilon_{\beta} e_{\beta}(x,y)\exp(i\beta z) d\beta$



When modeling wave propagation in an optical fiber, the most important modes are the guided modes and the leaky modes with low losses.



A mode solver makes it possible to determine the effectif index and the modal field distribution $\left(\vec{e},\vec{h}\right)$ of the modes for a given wavelength and a given transverse Refractive Index Profile (RIP that generally depends on wavelength due to material dispersion).

Using these two properties, we can also determine:

  • The effective area $A_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\left(\iint \vert e \vert^2 dS\right)^2}{\iint \vert e \vert^4 dS}$ and the non-linear coefficient
  • The group velocity $v_g=\left(\frac{\partial \beta}{\partial \omega}\right)^{-1}$
  • The modal/chromatic/polarization dispersion
  • The propagation losses : confinement losses $2\text{Im}(\beta)$, bend losses, surface scattering losses
  • Cut-off wavelength
  • Overlap with the core (for fiber amplifiers)
  • Fiber-coupling efficiency
  • Evolution of a beam during the propagation (in a graded-index fiber used in a mode field adapter for example)
  • etc.

  • Linear, homogeneous and isotropic medium for the core an cladding
  • No absorption, no magnetic properties ($\mu_r=1$)
  • Core: radius $a$, refractive index $n_1$
  • Cladding: infinite radius, refractive index $n_2$
  • Symmetry of revolution around the axis of the fiber $Oz$ and invariance by translation along $Oz$ (straight fiber)
  • Use of the cylindrical coordinate system $\left(r,\theta,z\right)$

Method to compute modes:

  1. Solve the equation of propagation of the EM field in each layer
  2. Link the two solutions using the interface conditions
  • Vector modes are the exact solutions of the Maxwell's equations.
  • For these modes, the fields are generally not transverse: $e_z$ and/or $h_z$ are different from zero
  • In each layer, the EM field satisfies the following equations of propagation: $$\left\{\begin{array}{ll} \Delta\vec{E}(\vec{r})+k_0^2n^2\vec{E}(\vec{r})=\vec{0} \\ \Delta\vec{H}(\vec{r})+k_0^2n^2\vec{H}(\vec{r})=\vec{0} \end{array}\right.$$

We only need to determine $e_z$ and $h_z$, since the other components of the fields are related to them by: $$\begin{array}{ll} &e_r=\frac{i}{n^2k_0^2-\beta^2}\left(\beta\frac{\partial e_z}{\partial r}+\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}}\frac{k_0}{r}\frac{\partial h_z}{\partial \theta}\right)\\ &e_{\theta}=\frac{i}{n^2k_0^2-\beta^2}\left(\frac{\beta}{r}\frac{\partial e_z}{\partial \theta}-\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}}k_0\frac{\partial h_z}{\partial r}\right)\\ &h_r=\frac{i}{n^2k_0^2-\beta^2}\left(\beta\frac{\partial h_z}{\partial r}-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0}{\mu_0}}\frac{k_0n^2}{r}\frac{\partial e_z}{\partial \theta}\right)\\ &h_\theta=\frac{i}{n^2k_0^2-\beta^2}\left(\frac{\beta}{r}\frac{\partial h_z}{\partial \theta}+\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0}{\mu_0}}k_0n^2\frac{\partial e_z}{\partial r}\right) \end{array}$$ $\Longrightarrow$ We will choose $e_z$ and $h_z$ purely imaginary such that $e_r$, $e_{\theta}$, $h_r$ and $h_{\theta}$ are real.

$2\pi$−periodicity in $\theta$ and interface conditions imply that ($\nu \in \mathbb{N}$ is the azimuthal number of the mode): $$e_z=\psi_e(r)\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\nu\theta)\\ \sin(\nu\theta) \end{pmatrix}\text{ and } h_z=\psi_h(r)\begin{pmatrix} -\sin(\nu\theta)\\ \cos(\nu\theta) \end{pmatrix}$$ The functions $\psi$ satisfy the differential Bessel equation: $$\boxed{\frac{\partial^2\psi(r)}{\partial r^2}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\psi(r)}{\partial r}+\left((k_0^2n^2-\beta^2)-\frac{\nu^2}{r^2}\right)\psi(r)=0}$$

If $(k_0^2n^2-\beta^2)>0$, solutions are the Bessel functions $J_{\nu}$ and $Y_{\nu}$.

If $(k_0^2n^2-\beta^2)<0$, solutions are the modified Bessel functions $I_{\nu}$ and $K_{\nu}$.

Guided modes:

  • Core: $\psi(r)=AJ_\nu\left(\frac{Ur}{a}\right)$ with $U=k_0a\sqrt{n_1^2-n_{\text{eff}}^2}$
  • Cladding: $\psi(r)=BK_\nu\left(\frac{Wr}{a}\right)$ with $W=k_0a\sqrt{n_{\text{eff}}^2-n_2^2}$

Interface conditions give: $$\boxed{\left(\nu n_{\text{eff}}\right)^2\left(\frac{V}{UW}\right)^4=\left(\frac{n_1^2J'_\nu(U)}{UJ_\nu(U)}+\frac{n_2^2K'_\nu(W)}{WK_\nu(W)}\right)\left(\frac{J'_\nu(U)}{UJ_\nu(U)}+\frac{K'_\nu(W)}{WK_\nu(W)}\right)}$$ where $V$ is the normalized frequency defined as: $$\boxed{V=k_0a\sqrt{n_1^2-n_2^2}=\sqrt{U^2+W^2}}$$ $\Longrightarrow$ We must determine the roots of this equation to find the effective indices of the guided modes.

Be careful, because a change in sign can indicate either a zero (and thus a mode) or a discontinuity.

As with vector modes, symmetry requires $e(r,\theta)=\psi(r)\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\ell\theta) \\ \sin(\ell\theta) \end{pmatrix}$ and $\psi$ satisfies the Bessel differential equation: $$\frac{\partial^2\psi(r)}{\partial r^2}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\psi(r)}{\partial r}+\left((k_0^2n^2-\beta^2)-\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}\right)\psi(r)=0$$ For a guided mode:

  • In the cladding: $\psi(r)=BK_{\ell}\left(\frac{Wr}{a}\right)$

For a leaky mode:

  • In the core: $\psi(r)=AJ_{\ell}\left(\frac{Ur}{a}\right)$
  • In the cladding: $\psi(r)=BH^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(\frac{U'r}{a}\right)$ with $U'=k_0a\sqrt{n_2^2-n_{\text{eff}}^2}$ and $H^{(1)}_{\ell}(x)=J_{\ell}(x)+iY_{\ell}(x)$ is a Hankel function

Interface conditions $\Longrightarrow$ $\boxed{U\frac{J'_\ell(U)}{J_\ell(U)}=U'\frac{H^{(1)'}_\ell(U')}{H^{(1)}_\ell(U')}}$

To find roots in the complex plane, you can use:

  1. Classical root finding methods in 2D space: Newton-Raphson, Müller, etc.
  2. Complex analysis-based root finding methods (which use Cauchy's argument principle): Delves-Lyness method, Global complex Roots and Poles Finding algorithm, etc.



  • For a step-index fiber, the confinement losses of leaky modes are very high
  • $\text{Im}(n_{\text{eff}})=10^{-6}$ at $\lambda=1$ µm corresponds to $\alpha\simeq 8.6$ dB/m
  • Guided modes: $n_N < n_{\text{eff}} < \max(n_i)$                             
  • Leaky modes: $\text{Re}(n_{\text{eff}}) < n_N$

Method:

  1. In the core (layer 1): $E\backsim J_{\ell}\left(\frac{U_1r}{a_1}\right)$
  2. Compute the field in layers 2 to N-1 with interface conditions
  3. In the cladding: $E\backsim K_{\ell}\left(\frac{W_Nr}{a_{N-1}}\right)$ (guided mode) or $E\backsim H^{(1)}_{\ell}\left(\frac{U_Nr}{a_{N-1}}\right)$ (leaky mode)
  4. The interface condition applied at $r=a_{N-1}$ gives an equation that can be used to determine $n_{\text{eff}}$


Note: the process to find the root may take a long time if there are many layers, especially for leaky modes $\Longrightarrow$ it is best to have an approximate value for the effective index to reduce computation time.

Example: mode LP02, $[n_1, n_2, n_3]=[1.44, 1.41, 1.43]$, $[a_1, a_2]=[4, 14]$ µm and $\lambda=1.6$ µm
      
Confinment losses decrease as the depth or thickness of the trench increases.

Maxwell's equations

  • $\text{curl}\left( \vec{E}\right)=-\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t}$
  • $\text{div} \left(\varepsilon \vec{E}\right)=0 \Longrightarrow \text{div} \left( \vec{E}\right)=-\text{grad} \left(\ln(\varepsilon) \right)\cdot\vec{E}$
  • $\text{curl}\left( \vec{B}\right)=\mu_0\varepsilon \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t}$
  • $\text{div}\left( \vec{B}\right)=0$

First method:

Discretize the core in layers which constant refractive index to obtain a multi-step index fiber. This method requires a large number of layers and is limited by the accuracy of the Bessel functions

There are, however, many other methods

$e_z=\psi_e(r)\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\nu\theta)\\ \sin(\nu\theta) \end{pmatrix}\text{ and } h_z=\psi_h(r)\begin{pmatrix} -\sin(\nu\theta)\\ \cos(\nu\theta) \end{pmatrix}$ where $\psi_e$ and $\psi_h$ satisfy a system of coupled differential equations: $$\left\{\begin{array}{ll} \frac{d^2 \psi_e}{d r^2}=\psi_e\left(\frac{\nu^2}{r^2}-k_0^2n^2+\beta^2\right)+\frac{d \psi_e}{d r}\left(\frac{2\beta^2}{\left(k_0^2n^2-\beta^2\right)n}\frac{d n}{dr}-\frac{1}{r}\right)-\psi_h\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}}\frac{2\nu\beta k_0}{r\left(k_0^2n^2-\beta^2\right)n}\frac{dn}{dr} \\ \frac{d^2 \psi_h}{d r^2}=\psi_h\left(\frac{\nu^2}{r^2}-k_0^2n^2+\beta^2\right)+\frac{d \psi_h}{d r}\left(\frac{2k_0^2n}{k_0^2n^2-\beta^2}\frac{d n}{dr}-\frac{1}{r}\right)-\psi_e\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_0}{\mu_0}}\frac{2\nu\beta k_0 n}{r\left(k_0^2n^2-\beta^2\right)}\frac{dn}{dr} \end{array}\right.$$ Shooting method to find guided and leaky modes:

  • The coupled equations is expressed in a linear first order differential equation: $\begin{pmatrix} \psi_e \\ \psi'_e \\ \psi_h \\ \psi'_h \end{pmatrix}' = A(r,\beta) \begin{pmatrix} \psi_e \\ \psi'_e \\ \psi_h \\ \psi'_h \end{pmatrix}$
  • To begin with, we use a thin layer between $r=0$ and $r=\epsilon\ll a$ with a constant refractive index: $\psi_e=J_{\nu}\left(\frac{Ur}{\epsilon}\right)$ and $\psi_h=\alpha J_{\nu}\left(\frac{Ur}{\epsilon}\right)$
  • A value of $n_{\text{eff}}$ is chosen and the system of differential equations is solved between $r=\epsilon$ and $r=a$ with a RK method for two values of $\alpha$ (generally 0 and 1).
  • By applying the interface conditions, we find a matching function that is zero if the value of neff corresponds to a mode

$h_r=A_r(r)\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\nu\theta)\\ \sin(\nu\theta)\end{pmatrix}$ and $h_{\theta}=A_{\theta}(r)\begin{pmatrix} \sin(\nu\theta)\\ \cos(\nu\theta)\end{pmatrix}$ where $A_r$ and $A_{\theta}$ satisfy a system of coupled differential equations: $$\left\{\begin{array}{ll} \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{dA_r}{dr}\right)-\frac{1+\nu^2}{r^2}A_r+k_0^2n^2A_r\pm\frac{2\nu}{r^2}A_{\theta}=\beta^2A_r \\ \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r\frac{d A_{\theta}}{dr}\right)-\frac{1}{r}\frac{d \ln(n^2)}{dr}\frac{d}{dr}\left(rA_{\theta}\right)-\frac{1+\nu^2}{r^2}A_{\theta}+k_0^2n^2A_{\theta}\pm \nu\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{d \ln(n^2)}{dr}+\frac{2}{r^2}\right)A_r=\beta^2A_{\theta} \end{array}\right.$$ Discrete Hankel transform to find guided modes:

  • $A_r$ and $A_{\theta}$ are expanded in a finite Fourier-Bessel series: $$f(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{N} f_iJ_n\left(\frac{j_{ni}r}{R}\right)$$ where $j_{ni}$ is the i-th root of $J_n$ and we assume that the field is zero for $r=R>a$
  • Apply the Hankel transform on the system of equations: $H\left(f(r)\right)=F(k)=\int_0^{\infty}rf(r)J_n\left(k r\right) dr$
  • This gives us an eigenvalue problem $Ax=\beta^2x$, which be solved to determine the modes

$e(r,\theta)=\psi(r)\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\ell\theta) \\ \sin(\ell\theta) \end{pmatrix}$ where $\psi$ satisfies the following differential equation: $$\frac{\partial^2\psi(r)}{\partial r^2}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\psi(r)}{\partial r}+\left((k_0^2n^2\textcolor{red}{(r)}-\beta^2)-\frac{\ell^2}{r^2}\right)\psi(r)=0$$
$\Longrightarrow$ analytical solution: if $n^2$ is parabolic, it is possible to perform a change of variables to reduce this equation to Kummer's equation whose solutions are confluent hypergeometric functions.

Otherwise:

  • Shooting method
  • Hankel transform
  • Finite difference or finite element method

Finite difference method:
$\Longrightarrow$ discretize the space with a step $h$ between $r=0$ and $r=R > a$ (for guided modes, we assume the Dirichlet boundary condition $E(R)=0$) $$\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}(r_i)\simeq \frac{\psi_{i+1}-\psi_{i-1}}{2h}\text{ and } \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial r^2}(r_i)\simeq \frac{\psi_{i+1}+\psi_{i-1}-2\psi_i}{h^2}$$ Take into account the boundary conditions at $r=0$:

  • For $\ell=0$, $\psi(r=0)=1$ and $\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}(0)=0$
  • For $\ell\neq 0$, $\psi(r=0)=0$ and $\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}(0)=1$

And this gives an eigenvalue problem: $$\begin{pmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ c_2 & a_2 & b_2 & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & c_3 & a_3 & \ddots & 0\\ \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & b_{N-1}\\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & c_N & a_N \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \psi_N \end{pmatrix}=\beta^2\begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \psi_N \end{pmatrix}\text{ with }\left\{\begin{array}{lll} a_i=-\frac{2}{h^2}+k_0^2n^2(r_i)-\frac{\ell}{r_i^2} \\ b_i=\frac{1}{h^2}+\frac{1}{2hr_i} \\ c_i=\frac{1}{h^2}-\frac{1}{2hr_i}\end{array}\right.$$

Finite element method:
By multiplying the equation satisfied by $\psi$ by $rv(r)$ where $v(r)$ is a test function ($v\in V=\left\{v\in H^1_r(0,R) \vert v(R)=0\right\}$), we obtain: $$\int_0^R\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\right)v(r)dr+\int_0^R\left(k_0n^2(r)r-\frac{\ell^2}{r}\right)\psi(r)v(r)dr=\beta^2\int_0^Rr\psi(r)v(r)dr$$ By integrating the first term in parts, we obtain the weak formulation: $$-\int_0^R r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}dr+\left[r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}v(r)\right]_0^R+\int_0^R\left(k_0n^2(r)r-\frac{\ell^2}{r}\right)\psi(r)v(r)dr=\beta^2\int_0^R r\psi(r)v(r)dr$$ Since v(R)=0, the boundary term is zero: $$\boxed{-\int_0^R r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}dr+\int_0^R\left(k_0n^2(r)r-\frac{\ell^2}{r}\right)\psi(r)v(r)dr=\beta^2\int_0^R r\psi(r)v(r)dr}$$ Then we apply the finite element method: in each element, $\psi=\sum_j u_j N_j(r)$ and $v=u_i(r)$ where $u_i$ are the shape functions (generally Lagrange polynomials). This gives a generalized eigenvalue problem: $$Au=\beta^2Bu$$

In both cases, we have to solve an eigenvalue problem:

  • $Au=\beta^2u$ for the finite difference method
  • $Au=\beta^2Bu$ for the finite element method

$A$ and $B$ are very large sparse matrices, and we don't need all the eigenvalues, but only those that correspond to the guided modes. There are various algorithms for computing eigenvalues close to an approximate value $\sigma$:

  • IRAM (Implicitly Restarted Arnoldi Method) used by Lumerical, Comsol Multiphysics, RSoft, Optiwave, FreeFEM++, eigs function of scipy (they use the ARPACK library)
  • Krylov-Schur used by eigs function of Matlab (since R2017b), ArnoldiMethod.jl package in Julia and SLEPc library (used by FEniCS)
  • Jacobi-Davidson implemented in SLEPc library


At each iteration, these algorithms need to solve $(A-\sigma I)x=w$ and generally use the LU factorization of the matrix $(A-\sigma I)$. There are also various algorithms/libraries for doing this: LAPACK, UMFPACK, MUMPS, PARDISO, CUDSS (for NVIDIA GPUs), etc.


But how do we calculate the leaky modes?

Leaky modes cannot be computed directly using FEM method because they are not bounded. Therefore it is impossible to find a value of R for which the field is zero.

Mathematically, leaky modes are analytic continuation of the guided modes below their cut-off $\Longrightarrow$ we can use an analytic dilatation (or complex scaling) of the space to compute leaky modes: $$\left\{\begin{array}{ll} \tilde{r}=r\text{ if } r \le r_{\text{PML}} \\ \tilde{r}=r+if(r) \text{ if } r > r_{\text{PML}} \end{array}\right.$$ $r_{\text{PML}}$ must be greater than $a$ and the real function $f$ must satisfy $f(r_{\text{PML}})=0$,must be positive and strictly increasing.
The layer located at $r>r_{\text{PML}}$ is named a Perfectly Matched Layer because there is no reflection at $r=r_{\text{PML}}$

The analytic continuation of $r$ can be written as $$\tilde{r}=\int_0^r s(\rho)d\rho$$ with $s(\rho)=1$ outside the PML and $s(\rho)=1+if'(\rho)$ in the PML (generally, s is a polynomial of degree 2 or 3).

The differential equation satisfied by $\tilde{\psi}(\tilde{r})$ is the same as $\psi(r)$: $\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}\frac{\partial \tilde{\psi}}{\partial \tilde{r}}\right)+\left((k_0^2n^2(\tilde{r})-\beta^2)-\frac{\ell^2}{\tilde{r}^2}\right)\tilde{\psi}(\tilde{r})=0$ $$\left\{\begin{array}{ll} n(\tilde{r})=n(r) \text{ if } r < r_{\text{PML}} \\ n(\tilde{r})=n_{\text{cladding}}=n(r) \text{ if } r > r_{\text{PML}} > a \end{array}\right.$$ By using the formula for differentiating composite functions i.e. $\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{r}}=\frac{1}{s(r)}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}$, we obtain this weak formulation: $$\boxed{-\int_0^R \frac{\tilde{r}}{s(r)}\frac{\partial\tilde{\psi}}{\partial r}\frac{\partial v^*}{\partial r}dr+\int_0^R\left(k_0n^2(r)\tilde{r}-\frac{\ell^2}{\tilde{r}}\right)s(r)\tilde{\psi}(r)v^*(r)dr=\beta^2\int_0^R \tilde{r} s(r)\tilde{\psi}(r)v^*(r)dr}$$ $\Longrightarrow$ We can now solve this eigenvalue equation in order to find leaky modes that are sufficiently attenuated by the PML.

  • The continuous spectrum rotates in the complex plane
  • The discrete spectrum includes some leaky modes that are revealed by the PML (in blue)

Differential equation: $\Delta e+k_0^2n^2(x,y)e=\beta^2 e$

Weak formulation for guided modes $\left(v\in V=\left\{v\in H^1(\Omega) \vert v=0 \text{ on } \partial\Omega \right\}\right)$: $$-\int_{\Omega}\vec{\nabla}(e) \cdot \vec{\nabla}(v) d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}k_0^2n^2ev d\Omega=\beta^2 \int_{\Omega} ev d\Omega$$ Weak formulation for leaky modes with a rectangular PML: $$-\int_{\Omega}\vec{\nabla}(\tilde{e}) \cdot (A \vec{\nabla}(v^*)) d\Omega+\int_{\Omega}k_0^2n^2\tilde{e}v^*S_xS_y d\Omega=\beta^2 \int_{\Omega} S_x S_y \tilde{e}v^* d\Omega\text{ with } A=\begin{pmatrix} \frac{S_y}{S_x} & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{S_x}{S_y} \end{pmatrix}$$

Guided modes:
$\vec{e}=\vec{e_t}+e_z\vec{u_z}$ and we can set $e'_z=\frac{1}{i\beta}e_z$ to obtain: $$\left\{\begin{array}{ll} -\vec{\nabla}\times\left(\vec{\nabla}\times \vec{e_t}\right)+\vec{\nabla}(e'_z)+k_0^2n^2\vec{e_t}=\beta^2\vec{e_t} \\ \vec{\nabla}\cdot\left(\vec{\nabla}(e'_z)\right)+k_0^2n^2e'_z=\beta^2\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{e_t} \end{array}\right.$$ We choose test functions $\vec{v}\in V=\left\{(\vec{v_t},v_z)\in H(\text{curl};\Omega)\times H^1(\Omega) \vert \vec{v}=\vec{0} \text{ on } \partial\Omega \right\}$ and we obtain a generalized eigenvalue problem of the form $Au=\beta^2Bu$

Leaky modes:
We have to replace $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ with $\frac{1}{s_x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial y}$ with $\frac{1}{s_y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}$ in Maxwell's equations in the case of rectangular PML.
And it's much more complicated in the case of a circular PML...

$\Longrightarrow$ We will use transform optics

Transform optics: in electromagnetism, when you change your coordinate system, we can keep the same equations but you have to replace your initial material properties ($\varepsilon$ and $\mu$) by equivalent material properties: $$\varepsilon'=\frac{J\varepsilon J^{T}}{\text{det}(J)}\text{ and }\mu'=\frac{J\mu J^{T}}{\text{det}(J)}$$ where $J$ is the jacobian matrix of the coordinate transformation consisting of the partial derivatives of the new coordinates with respect to the original ones: $J=\frac{\partial(x',y',z')}{\partial(x,y,z)}$.

The fields are also transformed: $$\left\{\begin{array}{ll} E'=J^{-T}E \\ D'=\frac{J}{\text{det}(J)}D \\ H'=J^{-T}H \\ B'=\frac{J}{\text{det}(J)}B\end{array}\right.$$

Application to the rectangular PML:

In the $(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},z)$ coordinate system, we have to solve the Maxwell's equations with the permittivity $\varepsilon_r=n^2(x,y)I$ and the permittivity $\mu_r=I$ where $I$ is the identity matrix.
If we want to compute the field in the real cartesian coordinate system, we have to use: $$\varepsilon_r'=n^2(x,y)T\text{ and }\mu_r'=T \text{ with }T=\frac{J J^T}{\text{det}(J)}$$ where $J=\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},z)}=\left(\frac{\partial(\tilde{x},\tilde{y},z)}{\partial(x,y,z)}\right)^{-1}$

Since $\tilde{x}=\int_0^x S_x(X)dX$ and $\tilde{y}=\int_0^y S_y(Y)dY$, $$J=\begin{pmatrix} 1/S_x & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1/S_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\\ \end{pmatrix}\Longrightarrow \epsilon'=n^2(x,y)\begin{pmatrix} S_y/S_x & 0 & 0\\ 0 & S_x/S_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\\ \end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\mu'=\begin{pmatrix} S_y/S_x & 0 & 0\\ 0 & S_x/S_y & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\\ \end{pmatrix}$$

$\Longrightarrow$ a PML is a fictive medium with anisotropic and complex permittivity and permeability
$\Longrightarrow$ to obtain the leaky and the guided modes, we can solve the Maxwell's equations with $\varepsilon'$ and $\mu'$ in a real cartesian coordinate system

$\Longrightarrow$ for a circular PML, $\varepsilon'$ and $\mu'$ are not diagonal

When using $\varepsilon'$ and $\mu'$, the weak formulation of the Maxwell's equation gives a quadratic eigenvalue problem: $Au+\beta B u +\beta^2 C u=0$
This can be solved by setting $v=\begin{pmatrix} u \\ \beta u \end{pmatrix}$, which reduces the problem to a standard eigenvalue problem: $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & I \\ -A & -B \end{pmatrix}v=\beta\begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & C \end{pmatrix}v$

Geometrical approach:

  • the bending implies that the angle of incidence decreases at the core/cladding interface located on the outer curvature side (and increases on the inner curvature side). These angles can no longer be sufficient to allow a total reflection $\Longrightarrow$ leak on the outer curvature side
  • The bending losses increase when the radius of curvature $R_c$ decreases (because $\theta_O$ decreases)
  • The bending losses increase with the order of the mode (because $\theta$ decreases)

Scalar case:

  • The bent fiber can be modelized by a straight fiber but of modified index profile with a higher index on the outer side of the curvature (because the optical path is longer).
  • The refractive index profile of the equivalent straight fiber is $n'=n\left(1+\frac{x}{R_c}\right)$

$\Longrightarrow$ When the fiber is bent, there are no guided modes in the strict sense of the term: the guided modes of the straight fiber become leaky modes in the bent fiber.

NB: If the elasto-optical effect is taken into account, we obtain $n_B(x,y)=n_S(x,y)\left(1+(1+\chi)\frac{x}{R_c}\right)$ where $\chi=-0.22$ for silica.
The negative sign indicates that the refractive index of silica decreases when it is in extension.

Note: the turning point must lie within the integration domain and, preferably, outside the PML.

Transform optics: The bent fiber is équivalent to a straight fiber but we have to replace $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ with $\varepsilon'$ and $\mu'$

$J^{-1}=\frac{\partial (x,y,z)}{\partial (x',y',z')}=\begin{pmatrix} \cos\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) & 0 & -\frac{x+R_c}{R_c}\sin\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) & 0 & \frac{x+R_c}{R_c}\cos\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} \cos\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) & 0 & -\sin\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) & 0 & \cos\left(\frac{z'}{R_c}\right) \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{x+R_c}{R_c} \end{pmatrix}$

$\varepsilon'=n^2\frac{JJ^T}{det(J)}=n^2\begin{pmatrix} 1+\frac{x}{R_c} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1+\frac{x}{R_c} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{1+\frac{x}{R_c}} \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mu'=\frac{JJ^T}{det(J)}=\begin{pmatrix} 1+\frac{x}{R_c} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1+\frac{x}{R_c} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{1}{1+\frac{x}{R_c}} \end{pmatrix}$


$\Longrightarrow$ A bent fiber can be modeled using a straight fiber whose permittivity and permeability are diagonal tensors.

How to compute $n_{\text{FSM}}$ ?
$\Longrightarrow$ Bloch theorem: $\vec{e}(x,y)=\exp\left(i\vec{k}_t\cdot \vec{r}\right)\vec{u}(x,y)$

  • $\vec{u}(x,y)$ has the same periodicity as the lattice
  • $\vec{k}_t$ is a vector of the Brillouin zone and is related to phase difference between an elementary lattice cell and its neighbors

$n_{\text{FSM}}$ corresponds to the fundamental mode, that is, the mode with the smallest transverse variations $\Longrightarrow$ computation at point $\Gamma$

Use FEM applied to the unit cell with periodic boundary conditions

Note: if the media are non-dispersive, there is a scale invariance: $n_{\text{FSM}}(h\lambda,hD,h\Lambda)=n_{\text{FSM}}(\lambda,D,\Lambda)$

Core modes can be computed by using FEM on the whole structure with a PML
